**[checked]** means the figure was read on the linked page this week. **[lead]** means it is from general knowledge and wants a source before the game states it as fact. Several of the long PDFs came back partly summarised by the fetching tool; where a number rests on a summary and not the page itself, it says so.

## Mavor, *General View of the Agriculture of Berkshire* (1809)

Read in full text from [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_fCaVWkVDljQC). The OCR is rough but the sums survive. All **[checked]**.

- **Rent.** "The average rent of farms throughout the county at 150l. a year, and of land of every description at about 1l. 1s." Pearce's report of 1794 had farms under £100.
- **The rise.** Rents on estates newly let are "at least 70 per cent" above what they fetched twenty years before.
- **The price of land.** Twenty-six to thirty-five years' purchase of a fair net rent. "The average, however, will not exceed twenty-eight."
- **Leases.** "Except some old leases of twenty-one years, nearly expired, I found few recent instances of so long a term." Fourteen is the longest granted in the last seven years; some run five, seven or ten. In the dairy country there are none, and Mr Loveden grants none at all.
- **The lodger.** Tenants at will, asked whether they would pay four or five shillings in the pound more for a lease, "hesitated". He compares such a tenant to a man calm in a storm that was bringing the house down, because he was "only a lodger".
- **Corn rents.** He proposes that landowners take them "the same as colleges and ecclesiastical bodies".
- **Owner-occupiers.** About a third of the county.
- **A farmer's profit.** Ten per cent on his capital is thought fair. And: "if a farmer cannot make full four rents of his crops in general, he is not on an equal footing with respectable tradesmen".
- **Wheat.** Four quarters an acre in the Vale of White Horse, two to two and a half on the light downs, three to four in the Kennet valley.
- **An acre of wheat.** Costs £11 13s, of which rent £1 10s. Returns £18 8s at nine shillings the bushel, straw included.
- **Tithe.** The highest rectorial composition is 7s an acre; several rectors take 4s in the pound of rent.
- **Poor rates.** About nine shillings in the pound on real rents in one parish, seven at Lambourn.
- **Wages.** "Perhaps only nine shillings a week", on which a man with young children "wholly" cannot keep them. The voice thread has Eden at 9s for Reading in 1795.
- **Inclosure.** Open-field property "would be worth fifty per cent. more to let" inclosed. Waste cannot be brought to the state of old inclosures for less than ten pounds an acre, poor land twelve to fourteen.
- **Coppice.** Cut at nine years, sold standing at ten to twenty-five pounds an acre.
- **Water meadows.** A guinea and a half to two and a half an acre for the spring feed, and the fold worth half a guinea more.
- **Wool.** Southdown at 50s the tod.

## Rents across the war

**Clark's charity-land series.** [checked], though the regional columns were aligned from OCR. Rent and tithe per acre, England: 18s in 1795 to 1799, 22s in 1800 to 1804, 26s in 1805 to 1809, 32s in 1810 to 1814, 29s in 1815 to 1819, 26s in 1820 to 1824. Berkshire in 1820 to 1824 stood at £1.23. Tithe took about 12 per cent of rent and tithe together nationally and 16 in the south-east. Common arable let about a quarter below inclosed, common waste nearly half below. ([Clark, "Land rental values and the agrarian economy"](https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/papers/rentereh.pdf).)

**Lord Darnley's Kentish estate.** [checked] The farms he held in 1788 rose 1.6 per cent a year to 1799, 1.8 to 1804, 5 per cent a year from 1805 to 1811, and not at all from 1812 to 1815. Knight's Place went from £80 in 1788 to £230 in 1809. Leases ran seven to twelve years, and farms were moved to tenancy at will between 1795 and 1812 to catch the rise. "After 1814 it was exceptional for a rent to be increased." Arrears passed £1,700 in 1815. Through the depression the nominal rent on a running lease stood and the landlord gave rebates instead, 15 to 30 per cent. Tenants in trouble "were not evicted". The home farm paid 1s 6d a day in 1790, 2s in 1797, 2s 6d in 1806 and 3s in 1812. ([Hunt, "Agricultural Rent in South-East England, 1788–1825"](https://www.bahs.org.uk/AGHR/ARTICLES/07n2a4.pdf).)

**Wiltshire next door.** [checked] Fourteen years was the common lease in 1794. Water meadows cost £3 to £20 an acre to make. After the war leases were said to bind only the landlord. ([VCH Wiltshire, vol. 4](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/wilts/vol4/pp65-91).)

## Inclosure

The historians disagree about almost everything except who got the money.

- Allen, working from Arthur Young's farms, finds no gain in efficiency worth the name and "a massive redistribution of income from farmers to landowners". [checked] ([Allen 1982](https://www.amherst.edu/media/view/107450/original/allen_1982_enclosure.pdf).)
- A recent study puts wheat yields 45 per cent higher in inclosed parishes and the land-value Gini up by 0.22. It cites Turner for a total cost of "at least 12 pounds per acre" with roads and fencing. [checked] ([Heldring, Robinson and Vollmer](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29772/w29772.pdf).)
- The public costs alone ran to "something over £2 an acre" before the wartime inflation, on J. M. Martin's figures as McCloskey reports them. This one rests on a summary of a paywalled page.

The design takes about £1,400 for the Act and award, and a thousand pounds to fence and build on the new farm.

## Money

**The usury ceiling.** The law "limited the rate of return on mortgages and bonds to 5 per cent, but did not limit the rates of return on government debt". [checked] ([Clark, "Debt, deficits and crowding out"](https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/210a/readings/debt2001.pdf).) Annuities fell outside it. A study of Irish lending finds them at a mean of 11.4 per cent between 1760 and 1799. That is Ireland, and the mean came from a summary, so the design's 14 per cent for an English annuity on a squire's life is a **[lead]**.

**Consol yields.** [checked] From the Bank of England's series as published by [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/LTCYUKA). Per cent: 4.52 in 1795, 4.88, 5.91 in 1797, 5.93, 5.09, 4.70 in 1800, 4.90, 4.23, 5.14 in 1803, 5.25, 5.07, 4.89, 4.77, 4.52, 4.38, 4.41 in 1810, 4.67, 5.01, 5.11, 4.52, 5.04 in 1815. The game's Consol prices are three divided by these. Above five per cent the Funds outbid the mortgage market. That mortgagees then called their money in is the standard account and a **[lead]** here: Clark's paper does not say it.

**The Bank Restriction.** Cash payments stopped on 25 February 1797, days after the French landed at Fishguard. [checked] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_Restriction_Act_1797).)

**A bank that broke.** Brown and Coombs of Windsor, ironmongers turned bankers, stopped on 13 February 1816 owing their London agents £23,870, with £17,789 of notes out. The partners went to Newgate and were released in August. [checked] ([Windsor Local History Group](https://windsorlocalhistorygroup.org/the-rise-and-fall-of-the-windsor-bank).) How many country banks there were and how many failed in 1814 to 1816 I could not reach; Pressnell's *Country Banking in the Industrial Revolution* is the book.

**Land tax redemption, 1798.** A proprietor could buy out his land tax by transferring Consols yielding a tenth more than the tax. **[lead]**

## The family

**Strict settlement.** [checked] The squire is tenant for life and his eldest son tenant in tail. When the son reached twenty-one he usually agreed to a new settlement in return for an income. That was the moment to fix jointures and portions and to square the estate's charges with its rental. A tenant for life was answerable for waste. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_settlement).) That waste covers felling timber, and that a jointure ran at about a tenth of the bride's portion, are both **[lead]**; Habakkuk's *Marriage, Debt and the Estates System* is the authority.

**Commissions.** The only table I reached is for 1837: ensign £450, lieutenant £700, captain £1,800, major £3,200, each step costing the difference. [checked] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchase_of_commissions_in_the_British_Army).) Prices in 1800 were somewhat lower. **[lead]**

## The county

All **[checked]** from the History of Parliament's 1790 to 1820 volumes.

- **[Wallingford](https://www.historyofparliamentonline.org/volume/1790-1820/constituencies/wallingford).** About 200 voters. The price of a vote "became stabilized at 20 guineas", handed out by a shoemaker known as the Miller. In 1817 a Member was reported to have sent 168 twenty-guinea notes.
- **[Reading](https://www.historyofparliamentonline.org/volume/1790-1820/constituencies/reading).** A hostile witness in 1809 put a seat at £4,000 at least in treats and douceurs, "and this without any man actually receiving a farthing by way of a bribe".
- **[Abingdon](https://www.historyofparliamentonline.org/volume/1790-1820/constituencies/abingdon).** Of some 240 electors, "about 70 of them take money".
- **[The county](https://www.historyofparliamentonline.org/volume/1790-1820/constituencies/berkshire).** Mr Loveden was said to be ready to spend £30,000 to carry it.

**The Speenhamland scale.** With the gallon loaf at a shilling, three shillings a week for a man and 1s 6d for his wife and each child, rising with the loaf. [checked] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speenhamland_system).)

**The Sheriff's year.** That it cost several hundred pounds and could not be declined is a **[lead]**.
