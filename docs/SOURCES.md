# Where the stories are

A working map of sources for Hatherley Combe: plotlines, events, colour, timelines. Compiled November 2026.

Two warnings before anything else. Every anecdote below carries a mark. **[checked]** means I read the linked page this week and the facts are as that page gives them. **[lead]** means it comes from general knowledge of the period and wants confirming before it goes into the game as fact. And several of the big free texts (Project Gutenberg, parts of archive.org, the National Archives catalogue) would not load from this workspace, so their entries say what they are and where they live, without quotation.

The voice thread is already gathering Cobbett, Burney, Austen's letters and Grose. Nothing here duplicates that. This is about what happened, not how people talked about it.

## The short answer

If only five things get opened, open these.

1. **The Annual Register, "Chronicle" section**, one volume per year. A day-by-day diary of the nation's oddities: fires, wagers, trials, storms, a pig of unusual size. Free on HathiTrust. It is the fastest way to fill a thin year.
2. **The Reading Mercury.** The county's own paper, on the British Newspaper Archive from 1767. Subscription, but worth a month's fee. Advertisements are the treasure: strayed horses, absconded apprentices, farms to let, quack medicines.
3. **Mavor's *General View of the Agriculture of Berkshire*** (1809, second edition 1813). A clergyman tours the county for the Board of Agriculture and reports on every tenant's drains. Page images on HathiTrust.
4. **History of Parliament Online, 1790 to 1820 volumes.** Free. A constituency essay for Berkshire and each of its boroughs, and a biography of every MP, written by people who enjoyed the gossip.
5. **Hammonds' *The Village Labourer*** (1911). Free on Gutenberg, ebook 69002. Partisan, furious and full of named cases.

## Berkshire itself

**Royal Berkshire Archives, Reading.** Quarter Sessions rolls and order books, enclosure awards, parish overseers' accounts, estate papers. The catalogue is searchable online; the documents mostly are not. Their blog is free and good, and it is where the 1809 flood below comes from. For the game: overseers' accounts give the price of a pauper's coffin and a pair of shoes for a bastard child, which is the sort of arithmetic Mr Crabbe would enjoy.

**Victoria County History of Berkshire** on British History Online. Free. Parish by parish: who held the manor, when the common went, which fair fell on which day. Dry, reliable, and the right place to steal a field name. East Ilsley, for instance, held sheep fairs on 1 and 26 August with a hiring fair in October, and once penned 80,000 sheep in a day. An old rhyme calls it "far famed for sheep and wool, tho' not for spinners", after a man named Hemsted failed to persuade the local women to spin.

**Berkshire Record Society volumes.** Printed editions of overseers' papers, enclosure records and the like. Paid, but libraries hold them.

**David Davies, *The Case of Labourers in Husbandry*** (1795). Davies was rector of Barkham, six miles from Reading, and he printed the weekly budgets of six of his own parishioners down to the halfpenny of thread. Free on HathiTrust. Nothing else tells you so exactly what the Pryors eat.

**Mary Russell Mitford.** Her letters from Bertram House near Reading run through 1806 to 1811 (L'Estrange's 1870 edition is on archive.org; a life is Gutenberg ebook 72028). Her father is a plotline on legs: a doctor who won a lottery fortune and spent the next twenty years losing it at cards and coursing. **[lead]** on the details; I could not open the text.

**Thomas Hughes, *The Scouring of the White Horse*** (1859, Gutenberg 53241). Old men's memories of the Uffington pastimes: cheese chased down the Manger, backsword play for a gold-laced hat, a gallon of gin for the longest smoker. Scourings are said to have fallen in 1803, 1808 and 1813 **[lead]**, which would drop one neatly into the thin stretch. Confirm the years in Hughes before building on them.

**Mrs Lybbe Powys's diaries**, 1756 to 1808 (archive.org). Gentry life at Hardwick House, across the Thames from Reading. Balls, theatricals, who dined with whom and what it cost. Henrietta's social calendar is in here.

## Year by year

**Annual Register** and the **Gentleman's Magazine**, both free on HathiTrust. The Register gives the national year; the Magazine gives obituaries, bankrupts, the weather and letters from clergymen about hedgehogs.

**Historic Hansard** (api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard). Free, searchable, begins 1803. Good for what the squire reads in the paper and gets cross about. Whitbread and Windham are reliably quotable.

**The London Gazette** (thegazette.co.uk). Free. Militia commissions, bankruptcies, the Gazette Extraordinary after a battle. If a neighbour is to go bankrupt, this is the page his name appears on.

**Free London papers on the BNA**: the Morning Chronicle, The Examiner (from 1808), The Statesman, Cobbett's Weekly Political Register. Registration needed, no charge. None of the Berkshire or neighbouring county titles is in the free set.

**Home Office papers, HO 42**, at the National Archives. Letters from frightened magistrates to the Home Secretary. Volunteers are cataloguing 1806 to 1811 now. The Archives' blog on 1810 notes hayrick burnings with rewards offered and the King's pardon dangled before accomplices. That is the `rickFire` event's paperwork.

**Tooke's *History of Prices*** (1838, free). The harvest and the wheat price for every year of the war. **[lead]**, from memory and to be checked against Tooke: annual average wheat ran near 75s a quarter in 1807, 81s in 1808, 97s in 1809, 106s in 1810, 95s in 1811 and 126s in 1812. The game's economy could follow that curve.

## Diaries, and three traps

**William Holland**, vicar of Over Stowey, Somerset, 1799 to 1818. Published as *Paupers and Pig Killers*. In copyright, cheap second-hand. Bad-tempered, observant, and the only one of the famous parson diaries that covers 1806 to 1811. He is Dr Gall's nearest living relation.

**John Skinner**, *Journal of a Somerset Rector*, 1803 to 1834. A miserable man among coal miners. In copyright.

**Joseph Farington's diary**, 1793 to 1821, on archive.org. London gossip from a painter who dined everywhere. Good for what reaches the country a fortnight late.

**Samuel Whitbread's justicing notebooks**, 1810–11 and 1813–14, edited by Alan Cirket for the Bedfordshire Historical Record Society. A magistrate's own record of every case that came to his door at Southill from December 1810, including, the editor says, matters "other justices would have thought trivial". He often summoned the other party for eight o'clock next morning. Paywalled on Cambridge Core. For a game in which the player sits as a JP, this is the single best model, and it sits inside the thin years.

**Joseph Mayett of Quainton**, a Buckinghamshire labourer who served in the militia from 1803 to 1815 and wrote it down. Buckinghamshire Record Society. The view from below, which the game's cottages need.

The traps. **Woodforde** stops in 1802. **Thomas Turner** of East Hoathly kept his diary from 1754 to 1765, so he is colour for the squire's father. And *The Diary of a Farmer's Wife 1796–97* by "Anne Hughes" is widely thought to be a twentieth-century confection. Charming. Do not cite it.

## Law, crime and the game laws

**Old Bailey Online.** Free and superb, but it is London and Middlesex only. Use it for how a trial sounds, and for Edmund's or Kitty's adventures in town. A Berkshire poacher went before Quarter Sessions or the Assizes at Reading and Abingdon, and those live in the archives and the Mercury.

**Douglas Hay and others, *Albion's Fatal Tree*** (1975). Poaching on Cannock Chase, anonymous threatening letters, the theatre of the assize. In copyright. Read Hay's essay on property and authority before writing any scene on the bench.

**P. B. Munsche, *Gentlemen and Poachers*.** The game laws explained properly. Mudge's reading list.

**Lawrence Stone, *Road to Divorce*** and *Broken Lives*. Crim. con. actions, with the servants' evidence. See Mr Loveden below.

## Historians worth the money

**Jenny Uglow, *In These Times*** (2014). Britain at home through the wars, year by year, built from letters and diaries of bankers, farmers and mill girls. Closest in spirit to the game.

**Clive Emsley, *British Society and the French Wars 1793–1815*.** Short and chronological. A skeleton timeline in paperback.

**Amanda Vickery, *The Gentleman's Daughter*.** Lancashire gentry women, their account books and their servants problem. For Henrietta and Kitty.

**E. P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class*** and *Customs in Common*. The "moral economy" essay in the second is the theory behind the game's `flour` event. *Whigs and Hunters* is about Windsor Forest, though seventy years early.

**Roger Wells, *Wretched Faces*.** The famines of 1795 and 1800. **J. M. Neeson, *Commoners*.** What a common was actually worth to a cottager.

## Material for 1806 to 1811

**The great flood, January 1809. [checked]** On 19 January rain froze as it touched the ground; snow lay on the ice until the 24th, then thawed in a day. The curate of Sutton Courtenay wrote in his register that it was "the highest flood ever recorded", fifteen inches above 1775. Boats went from the Swan public house to Abingdon, people were fetched from their bedrooms by boat, "many cattle drowned", and the church saw no baptism, wedding or burial for three weeks. Three arches of Wallingford Bridge were rebuilt from 1810 after a flood. ([Royal Berkshire Archives](https://www.royalberkshirearchives.org.uk/blog/flooding-extreme), [Wallingford Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallingford_Bridge).) *Hook:* the water meadows, the tenant's ewes, and a bridge the county expects the squire to subscribe to.

**The Newbury Coat, 25 June 1811. [checked]** Sir John Throckmorton bet a thousand guineas that John Coxeter of Greenham Mills could turn wool on the sheep at sunrise into a coat on his back by sunset. Two sheep were shorn at five in the morning. The cloth was done by four. James White and nine tailors finished at twenty past six, and Sir John sat down to dinner with forty gentlemen in a damson-coloured hunting coat. Five thousand people watched. The two sheep were roasted and 120 gallons of strong beer went round. Accounts differ on whether it took thirteen hours ten or twenty, and on who bought the beer. ([Berkshire History](https://berkshirehistory.com/articles/newburycoat.html), [MoneyWeek](https://moneyweek.com/397571/25-june-1811-the-throckmorton-coat-challenge).) *Hook:* Hartopp proposes Hatherley wool can do it faster. The player may back him, bet against him, or supply the sheep.

**Two canals in one autumn, 1810. [checked]** The Wilts and Berks reached Abingdon on 22 September; the proprietors' boat passed the last lock at half past two and they dined in the Council Chamber until late. It had cost £255,262 against an estimate of about £112,000, and shares first offered at £100 ended at £12 10s. The Kennet and Avon finished with no ceremony at all: on the evening of Friday 28 December a single barge of stone for the Earl of Ailesbury climbed Caen Hill. On the Monday two coal boats followed and a third stuck in the frost. Carriage from London to Bath fell to £2 9s 6d a ton, against £6 3s or more by road. ([Wilts and Berks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilts_%26_Berks_Canal), [K&A Canal Times](https://kacanaltimes.blogspot.com/2010/12/on-evening-of-friday-28th-december-1810.html), [Kennet and Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennet_and_Avon_Canal).) *Hook:* the game's `canal` event closes in 1805. Here is its reckoning: cheap Somerset coal for the cottages, and shares worth an eighth of what the squire paid.

**The gentlemen Danes of Reading, 1807 to 1814. [checked]** After Copenhagen, nearly 600 Danish and Norwegian prisoners passed through Reading on parole, two or three hundred at a time, mostly merchant skippers and mates. The agent, Herbert Lewis, ignored the town limits and the curfew. They joined the Odd Fellows. The young officers held a weekly dance in a cellar with their washerwomen's daughters, on weak punch, with a washerwoman as chaperone, and at least three married. Seven died, two by their own hand. When the merchant Laurentius Braag died in September 1808 the town raised nearly £200. ([Reading Museum](https://www.readingmuseum.org.uk/blog/unearthing-gentlemen-danes-st-laurence-churchyard).) *Hook:* a companion or rival for Lieutenant Dubreuil, and a cellar Kitty should not know about.

**Mr Loveden of Buscot, 1808 onwards. [checked]** Edward Loveden Loveden, former MP for Abingdon, Thames commissioner, Board of Agriculture man and once Lieutenant-Colonel of the Berkshire Militia, separated in 1808 from his third wife Anne, twenty-two years his junior. She was alleged to have taken up with a Mr Barker, Fellow of Merton, and Loveden's own servants gave the evidence. He won his crim. con. action, but the divorce was never completed, and when told he must pay her £400 a year he petitioned the Lords against it as a reward for adultery. He had also driven off one daughter's suitor. ([Wikipedia, citing the History of Parliament and Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Loveden_Loveden).) *Hook:* a neighbour's scandal in which the player's footman is asked to testify.

**A song about bread, 1811. [checked]** The Liverpool Local Militia complained of their bread. Private Thomas Taylor, a married clerk, wrote a song on the subject and had it printed. He was tried the same day with one hour to prepare, sentenced to 200 lashes and given 50. His wife fainted at the first stroke. Burdett raised it in the Commons on 25 May; Whitbread called flogging "wholly unnecessary in the English army". ([Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1811/may/25/flogging-in-the-local-militia-taylors).) Two years earlier at Ely, local militiamen who mutinied over stoppages from their pay were ringed by German Legion cavalry and five were sentenced to 500 lashes. Cobbett wrote about it and was convicted of seditious libel in June 1810, then sent to Newgate for two years. ([Past Tense](https://pasttense.co.uk/2016/06/15/today-in-publishing-history-radical-journo-william-cobbett-tried-for-objecting-to-flogging-in-militia-1810/).) *Hook:* the squire holds a Local Militia commission, and a Hatherley man has written a song. It also gives the `cobbett` event a second act.

**The Local Militia itself, 1808–09. [checked]** Castlereagh told the Commons in March 1809 that 250 regiments held 195,161 men, most of them old Volunteers, and proposed cutting the two-guinea bounty to one. Whitbread warned that volunteering would stop and men would have to be balloted. Windham said the army's "greatest merit belonged to its taylors". ([Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1809/mar/27/local-militia-bill).) Berkshire raised three regiments, and there is a report of a disturbance at Reading in 1809 **[lead]**. Follows on from `edmundHome`.

### Leads, unchecked

All from general knowledge. Each needs a source before use.

- **1806.** Pitt dies in January, Fox in September. A general election in the autumn and another in spring 1807 on the cry of "No Popery". Two writs for Wyvern in seven months is expensive.
- **1808.** County meetings denounce the Convention of Cintra. Does the squire sign the address?
- **1809.** Captain Barclay walks a thousand miles in a thousand hours at Newmarket for a thousand guineas. The Walcheren expedition sends men home with a fever that lingers for years: a subject for one of Edmund's letters. The Old Price riots at Covent Garden.
- **1810.** Burdett goes to the Tower in April; he had a seat at Ramsbury, just over the Wiltshire border. A run of country bank failures in the summer. Princess Amelia dies at Windsor in November and the King's mind goes with her.
- **1811.** The Regency begins in February. The second census. A great comet hangs over the harvest from September and gets blamed for everything. The Merino Society is founded as the craze peaks.
- **Prize-fighting.** Cribb beats Molineaux in December 1810 and again in September 1811. Lord Cullerne would have money on it.
