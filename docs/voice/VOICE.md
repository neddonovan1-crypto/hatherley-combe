# The Hatherley hand

How the game talks. Read this before you write or alter a word the player will see, then run `node tools/voicecheck.js`.

Companions: [LEXICON.md](LEXICON.md) for money, titles, dates and vocabulary; [SAMPLES.md](SAMPLES.md) for events done over, old beside new; [corpus/](corpus/) for the period itself.

## What was wrong, three times

The narrator stood in our century. He explained the game laws. He knew the Speenhamland scale would last forty years.

Then nearly every sentence had the same build. A statement. A comma. Then a comment hung on the end to be clever about the statement. Ned put two of them side by side:

> over a turbot that cost more than a ploughman earns in a month, to decide what a ploughman ought to earn in a month. Nobody sees the joke.

> A man with a wife and three children has eight shillings a week to buy it with, and the gentlemen present are too good arithmeticians to pretend that the sum comes out.

The second has better words in it. It is the same sentence.

The cure for that was taken too far. The whole game was written in sentences of six and eight words, one fact apiece, and a historian who read it said the plain thing: "Main thing that stands out though is the staccato sentences. Needs that 18thC prose!" A page of 1795 does not go Fact. Fact. Fact. It goes in periods.

## The period

The narrator's unit is the period: a sentence of some length, built of members joined by semicolons and by "and", "but", "for", "so that", with its subordinate matter placed early and its main fact kept back for the end.

> It is four in the morning when Henrietta wakes you; and as she has a candle in her hand, and has not stayed to put on her cap, you are sitting up before she speaks.

> The Captain, it appears, sat down to hazard at the first change of horses, with a view to mending the journey money; and has lost, in the order named, the journey money, the curricle, and Kitty's pearl ear-rings.

How it is made:

- **Suspend.** Put the circumstance, the reason or the concession first ("As the best bonnet is gone from its box, and the second-best left behind, ..."), and let the main clause arrive last.
- **Balance.** Two or three members of like weight, parted by semicolons: "Wheat stands at seventy-five shillings the quarter; the quartern loaf is elevenpence in Newbury market; and a labourer in this county, when he can find a master, has eight shillings a week with which to buy it."
- **Embed.** The relative clause sits in the middle, between subject and verb, where it cannot dangle: "Mr Hartopp, who was never known to waste either a pencil or a shilling, has the first of these out before the cheese comes."
- **Connect.** The period's own joiners: "and as", "for", "nor", "upon which", "it appears", "so that", "by which hour", "in the order named", "having", "being". "Upon" for "on" where a hand rests or a day falls. Not "however" at the head of a sentence, and none of the checker's banned connectives.
- **Land on a fact.** The last words of a period are the heaviest. Give them a sum, a name, an object, a thing done. "... that he recollects to touch his hat."

A short sentence is now an instrument and no longer the rule. After two long periods one plain line tells: "Nobody stirs." "From Hatherley to Gretna Green is three hundred and twenty miles." Three short sentences running is the old fault come back, and the checker will say so.

Do not write a paragraph as one sentence, and do not let two paragraphs running have the same build.

## The hung clause

This rule stands, and the period is how it is kept.

A sentence may be long. It may not report a thing and then turn round and remark on it. Forms to refuse:

- "..., and [dry observation]."
- "..., which is [deflation]."
- "..., as [comparison that explains the joke]."
- A short knowing sentence after a paragraph, doing the same work from outside: "Nobody sees the joke."

The test is where the wit sits. In a hung clause it trails after the fact, apologising. In a period it goes in front or in the middle, and the fact comes down on top of it. Not "an ostler brings your horse, though he plainly knows who you are" but "under the open window of the taproom, your horse is brought round by an ostler whom you do not know."

A last member that carries the action forward is no tail: "He writes upon the back of his half-sheet, turns it about, and pushes it across the cloth to you."

## The narrator

Second person, present tense. Ned has ruled on both.

Inside the year. He knows what Wyvern knows on market day. Nothing "for the rest of his life". The Reading Mercury at the end may look back, and only from 1815.

He does not explain. The player learns the penalty for a hare because the Act is open on the table.

He is allowed one general remark to a paper, in the manner of Fielding: "as no business of the county was ever yet opened upon an empty stomach, they sit down first to a turbot." One, and no more; placed before the main clause, never after it; and about mankind or the county, never about the squire's own heart. Many papers should have none.

Otherwise he has eyes and ears. He says who stood where, what they held, what was said, what it cost, what they did with their hats. The wit belongs to the people in the room, to the arithmetic, and to the order in which a period lets its facts fall.

Period feeling comes from the syntax and from the nouns together (a peck measure, a tailboard, a draft on Tidmarsh's bank). Capitalised Nouns remain fancy dress in the narrator; they belong to the prints and the pocket-book.

Dialogue carries a great deal. Write it as lines, each on its own. Gentry and clergy may speak in periods. Labourers, servants and Lord Cullerne do not.

Read a page of the corpus before you begin: Austen's letters or *Humphry Clinker* for the run of a sentence, Gilbert White for calm, Cobbett for a period that is angry and still stands up.

## Five hands, not one

Text reaches the player in one of five hands, and they should not sound alike. The narrator and the letter-writers of rank write in periods. The pocket-book, the handbill and the poor do not, and the difference is the texture of the game.

**The narrator.** Second person, present tense, in periods. See above.

**The letter.** Edmund from his ship, Kitty from Northampton, Tidmarsh, the Lord Lieutenant's clerk. An educated letter is the most periodic thing in the game: "I will not pretend that I have yet learnt much divinity; but I have made myself perfect master of one branch of knowledge, which is, how to cross the quadrangle in such a manner that the Provost shall not see my boots." A letter has a place and a day at its head, a salutation, and a subscription, and the subscription is a gauge of feeling: when Edmund moves from "your dutiful son" to "your affectionate son" something has happened, and nobody need say what. Letters are charged to whoever receives them, by the sheet, which is why the young cross theirs and the rich get them franked.

**The public prints and the wall.** Handbills, the Mercury, notices of sale, a board in a wood. These have formulas and should keep them: WHEREAS, the capitals on the Nouns, "on Tuesday se'nnight", "none but principals will be treated with". A reward bill quoted whole does more than a paragraph telling the player a reward was offered. Capitalised nouns belong here and in the pocket-book only. In the narrator they are fancy dress.

**The pocket-book.** The squire's own memoranda, for quiet seasons. Write these against the real Woodforde in the corpus, not from memory of him. He gives his Nouns capitals. He writes "this Morn'", "to day", "&c.", "rosted", "recd." He says what was for dinner and which way the wind sat. His entries are clipped, as a real diary of the 1790s was, and they stay clipped. Money goes at the end of the sentence, ruled as he ruled it: "To a Fisherman for a Pike paid this Morn' 0. 1. 6." No wit is attempted. Once in a long while a feeling gets one flat line to itself: "Nancy I think ought not to be so to me."

**Unlettered writing.** Jem Pryor's notes, a servant's letter. The model is Win Jenkins in *Humphry Clinker*, who writes "Heaving this importunity" for "having this opportunity". Spell by ear. Each mistake must be one a real ear would make.

**Speech.** By rank, and each person with a habit. Lord Cullerne drops his g's and says "ain't", as a Whig earl of his generation did, and never explains himself. Dr Gall speaks in periods and quotes Scripture against people. Crabbe is all respect and subordinate clauses. Hartopp has caught his vocabulary from Arthur Young and cannot see a field without "capabilities". Jem Pryor says little, and what he says is Berkshire: "her" for she, "I be", "un" for him, a "z" now and then. One mark of dialect to a speech is enough. Mrs Bunce does not speak; things are reported of her. Keep that.

## How to write an event

Do not revise old prose. It carries its skeleton with it, and the skeleton is the fault.

1. Reduce the event to beats on a scrap: who, where, what is asked, each choice, what follows each choice, which flags and sums move. No phrases from the old text.
2. Close the old text.
3. Write from the beats. Ask what a person standing in the room would see and hear. Then ask which fact each paragraph exists to deliver, and build the period so that it comes last.
4. Put the code back round it exactly as it was.

## Where the comedy goes

Into what people do and what things cost. Five pounds or three months, for a hare. Four shillings a week for thirteen weeks out of the rates. A miller holding his own hat with his own money in it.

One simile to an event, or none. Some outcomes should not be funny at all.

## Never

- Em dashes or en dashes. Commas, semicolons, colons, full stops.
- genuinely, straightforward, moreover, furthermore, "it is worth noting", and the rest of the checker's list.
- The hung clause, above.
- Three short sentences running in the narrator.
- The three-part list with a twist in the tail.
- Paragraphs of equal length marching down the card.
- Explaining a custom the characters would take for granted.
- Americanisms and American spelling.
- A word in the checker's period list without a reason you could defend to someone with the OED open.

## Before you commit

0. Did you write it from beats, with the old text shut?
1. Does it read like a page of 1795 or like a telegram? Count the full stops in a paragraph. Then: which of the five hands is this in? If "narrator" for the fourth event running, find a letter or a bill in it.
2. Read it aloud. Where you perform, cut.
3. Check every sum against LEXICON.md and every title against who the person is.
4. `node tools/voicecheck.js`. Errors must be mended. Advice may be overruled, with a reason.
5. `./build.sh` and `node test/sim.js 500`. Prose changes must not touch a flag, a number or a name.
