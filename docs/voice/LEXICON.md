# Lexicon

Working notes for 1795 to 1815, southern England, gentry and parish. Compiled from general knowledge of the period, not yet checked line by line against the corpus. Where a date of first use is given it is approximate. Correct this file when the sources say otherwise.

## Money

Twelve pence to the shilling, twenty shillings to the pound, twenty-one to the guinea.

- **Guineas** for anything a gentleman would be ashamed to pay in pounds: a physician, a horse, a wager, a picture, a subscription, a bribe. **Pounds** for rent, land, wages, debts, taxes.
- Coins the squire handles: guinea, half-guinea, the seven-shilling piece (from 1797), crown, half-crown, shilling, sixpence, and Mr Boulton's great copper "cartwheel" pennies and twopences of 1797. Halfpence and farthings below.
- **No sovereigns.** The coin does not exist until 1817.
- After the Bank Restriction of February 1797 gold goes to ground. People hoard guineas; the Bank issues one and two pound notes for the first time; country banks print their own, and a note is only as good as the banker. Spanish dollars with the King's head stamped on them pass for want of silver. From 1811 there are Bank tokens. A sentence like "in guineas, while there are guineas to be had" carries all this.
- A fortune is stated as income: "two thousand a year". A woman's as capital: "ten thousand pounds", which in the funds means four or five hundred a year. "The three per cents" are Consols. A mortgage is at five per cent, the legal ceiling.
- Sums in the narrator are words: "a hundred and forty pounds", "seven shillings in the pound", "two pounds twelve". In a document they are figures in the period's manner: `£140`, `140l.`, `7s. 6d.`, or as Woodforde ruled his page, `0. 2. 6`.
- Rough scale. Farm labourer, seven to nine shillings a week in 1795 (Eden found 9s. at Reading that September) and nine to twelve by 1809 (Mavor). Maidservant, five to eight pounds a year and her tea. Footman, eight to fifteen and livery. A curate, forty or fifty pounds. A gentleman begins at about three hundred a year. The quartern loaf (about four pounds and a third) runs from sixpence in a cheap year past a shilling and fourpence in 1800 and 1812. Eden has it at 11½d. in Reading in September 1795.
- Bankruptcy pays "so many shillings in the pound". A bill is "discounted". A man in difficulties is "embarrassed", "done up", "under water", or has "outrun the constable".

## The year

- Quarter days: Lady Day (25 March), Midsummer (24 June), Michaelmas (29 September), Christmas. Rents fall at Lady Day and Michaelmas, and farms change hands then.
- "Tuesday last", "Tuesday se'nnight" (a week ago Tuesday, or a week come Tuesday, by context), "this day fortnight", "the 14th instant" (this month), "ultimo" (last), "proximo" (next).
- "The year '95." "These ten years." "Since King Charles's time."
- Dinner is the main meal and is eaten at three or four in the country, later in town and later as the period goes on. Breakfast about nine or ten. Tea after dinner. Supper for those still up. A dinner has "courses" and "removes", and everything in a course is on the table at once.

## Names and titles

- **Mr** for a gentleman, an attorney, a farmer of substance, a steward addressed. The squire speaks of his steward as plain "Crabbe" and of a labourer by both names or the first. "Esquire" goes after the full name in print and on the outside of a letter.
- The eldest unmarried daughter is "Miss Ashcombe"; a younger one "Miss Kitty". A wife speaks of and to her husband as "Mr Ashcombe" before anyone but the children.
- A peer is "Lord Cullerne" in the third person, "my lord" or "your lordship" to his face, "the Earl" in a newspaper. His wife is "Lady Cullerne", "her ladyship". Only the children of peers are "the Honourable", and of earls only the younger sons. **Fitzroy Bligh, as Lord Cullerne's nephew, is "the Honourable" only if his own father was a peer.** The game should either say so once or drop the style.
- A doctor of divinity is "Dr Gall", "the Doctor". A physician is also "Dr"; a surgeon or apothecary is "Mr".
- Army and Militia ranks are used socially: "Captain Vane". Navy lieutenants are "Mr" aboard ship.
- "Serjeant", "gaol", "waggon" or "wagon" (pick one: the game has "wagon"), "inclosure" in the Act and "enclosure" in talk, "shew" and "chuse" in documents only.
- Servants: upper servants by surname (Mrs Bunce, whether married or not), footmen by Christian name, and often a name the family finds convenient.

## Letters

- Head: place and day. "Sheerness, Sunday." "Hatherley, 14th June."
- Open: "Sir," to a stranger or an inferior on business; "Dear Sir," to an acquaintance; "My dear Father"; "My dear Madam".
- Body: "I take up my pen", "your favour of the 3rd inst." (your letter), "I beg leave to", "pray", "I am sorry to say", "you will be glad to hear".
- Close, in rising warmth: "I am, Sir, your most obedient humble servant" / "your obliged and faithful servant" / "your dutiful son" / "your affectionate son" / "ever yours". "Yrs &c." between men who are busy.
- Furniture: the "direction" (address), a wafer or a seal, a frank (a peer or Member's signature that carries it free), "charged double" for a second sheet, "crossed" writing to save one, "by the carrier", "by express" when it cannot wait.

## The prints

Formulas to keep whole.

- `WHEREAS ...; whoever will discover the Offender or Offenders ... shall on Conviction receive ...`
- `TO BE SOLD BY AUCTION, by Mr Skinner, at the George Inn, Wyvern, on Thursday the 3d of October, at Eleven o'Clock in the Forenoon (unless previously disposed of by private Contract)`
- `STOLEN or STRAYED, out of a Close at ...`
- `ABSCONDED, and left his Family chargeable to the Parish of ...`
- `WANTS a Place, as ..., a steady Man who can have an undeniable Character from his last.`
- `MARRIED. On Monday last, at ..., Mr —, to Miss —, an agreeable young Lady with a genteel Fortune.`
- `DIED. On Tuesday se'nnight, in the 73d Year of his Age, universally lamented ...`
- News: "We hear from Newbury that", "A correspondent informs us", "It is confidently reported", "Yesterday arrived the mail from".
- `N.B.` `Letters (post paid).` `None but Principals will be treated with.`
- The Riot Act proclamation begins: "Our Sovereign Lord the King chargeth and commandeth all persons, being assembled, immediately to disperse themselves, and peaceably to depart to their habitations, or to their lawful business."

## Words that have moved

Use them in the old sense and the period arrives without being announced.

| Word | Then |
|---|---|
| nice | fastidious, exact |
| candid | generous in judging others |
| condescension | a virtue in the great: affability to inferiors |
| sensible | aware ("I am sensible of the honour"); also capable of feeling |
| want | lack ("the gate wants hanging") |
| interest | influence, patronage ("he has interest at the Admiralty") |
| character | a servant's reference; a reputation |
| address | manner, bearing |
| intelligence | news |
| competence, independence | enough money to live on without working |
| expectations | money one hopes to inherit |
| situation, place | a post; "out of place" is unemployed |
| warning | notice to quit |
| establishment | a household and its scale |
| connexion | relations, and their usefulness |
| amiable, agreeable, genteel | praise; "genteel" is already a little vulgar by 1810 |
| tolerable | fairly good |
| the Faculty | doctors |
| the people | servants or labourers, according to who is speaking |
| mechanic | a man who works with his hands |

## Talk

- Gentry: "I dare say", "upon my word", "depend upon it", "I make no doubt", "to be sure", "pray", "a vast deal", "I am persuaded", "I had as lief", "out of spirits", "in a taking", "put about", "low", "bilious".
- Oaths, mild to strong: "Lord!", "la" (girls), "the deuce", "egad" and "zounds" (old men only by 1800), "damme". Print damns as `d—d` in a newspaper and whole in speech.
- Dismissal: "stuff", "fudge", "fiddlestick", "gammon", "humbug".
- Money slang: "blunt", "the ready", "rhino". A creditor "duns". A dupe is "a flat", his plucker "a sharp" or "a leg".
- Drink: "foxed", "disguised", "cut", "in his cups", "half seas over". A full glass is "a bumper", the dregs "heel-taps".
- The enemy: "Boney", "the Corsican", "Mounseer", "Johnny Crapaud". At home: "Mr Pitt", "Billy Pitt"; "Farmer George" for the King; "Prinny" late.
- To ignore someone in public is to "cut" him. To inform is to "peach". To be dismissed is to be "turned off" or "turned away".
- Travel: the mail, the stage, a post-chaise ("to post" is to travel by one), a gig, a curricle, a chariot, a tax-cart. Ostlers, post-boys, the turnpike. An inn has a "tap" for the lower sort and an "ordinary" at a fixed price.

Grose's *Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue* (1811) is the authority for the low words. `tools/fetch-corpus.sh` brings it into `corpus/full/`. The list above was checked against it on 27 November 2026:

- In Grose as given: blunt, the ready, rhino, dun, flat, sharper, foxed, disguised, cut (drunk), half seas over, bumper, heel-tap, fudge, humbug, peach, fiddlestick's end.
- Grose has "blacklegs" whole, for a sharper on the turf or in the cockpit. The short "a leg" is a few years later. Before 1810 write "a blackleg".
- Grose spells it "gamon" and gives it as a verb: to humbug, to tell lies. "What rum gamon" is thieves' talk. Keep it out of a gentleman's mouth.
- To "cut" an acquaintance is marked a Cambridge word. Give it to the young and to those who copy them.
- Boney, Mounseer, Johnny Crapaud and Prinny are not cant and are not in Grose. They belong to the print shops and the newspapers.

## Berkshire speech

Light. One mark in a speech.

- "I be", "we be", "thee bist" among themselves.
- "Her" for she, "un" for him or it: "I seed un." "Her wants four days' hanging."
- "Zur", "zummat", "vour": the southern z and v, used once and then left.
- "A-going", "a-coming". "Afeard". "Housen" for houses. "Main" and "terrible" to mean very: "main cold".
- "Ent" for isn't, which the game already has on the keeper's board.
- Written by the unlettered: phonetic, short, capitals for emphasis, as in the Ludd letter. Never comic misspelling for its own sake; each error should be one a man would make spelling by ear.

## Not yet invented, or not yet meant

Avoid in any hand: scientist; weekend; lunch (luncheon and nuncheon are safe); bogeyman; cost of living; living wage; police and detective; blackmail (say hush-money); envelope and postage stamp; lucifer matches (a tinder-box); sideburns (whiskers); cab; boss; staff for servants; okay and hello; deadline, feedback, background, contact as a verb, research as a verb; nostalgia, empathy, paranoia, mesmerise; boycott, sabotage, hooligan.

Things that do exist and are worth using: the optical telegraph on its hills; vaccination from 1798; gas lit in Pall Mall in 1807; the steam engine as a pump and a threshing-machine as a novelty; the Census; income tax; canals; the comet of 1811; trousers creeping in on young men after 1800 while their fathers keep breeches.
