# Old beside new

Six events and eight quiet-season entries have been done over in the Hatherley hand: `speen`, `flour`, `nore`, `thorneLease`, `poacher`, `rickFire`, and two vignettes for each season. Nothing mechanical moved. Three are set out here; the rest are in `src/`.

## The Pelican at Speen (narrator, no hindsight)

**Before**

> The Berkshire bench has met at the Pelican Inn at Speen, over a turbot that cost more than a ploughman earns in a month, to decide what a ploughman ought to earn in a month. Nobody sees the joke.
>
> Dr Gall proposes that wages be left where they are and the parish make up the difference out of the rates, by a scale tied to the price of the gallon loaf: so much for a man, so much for his wife, so much for every child. “It costs us nothing,” he says, which is untrue, “and it keeps them quiet,” which may not be.

and, if you second him:

> ...You have helped build, over one dinner, a machine for turning labourers into paupers. It will run for forty years, and the turbot was overcooked.

**After**

> The magistrates of the county are met at the Pelican at Speen, by advertisement, to consider the state of the labouring poor, and have begun, as the bench does generally begin, with the turbot. Bread is at elevenpence the quartern. A man with a wife and three children has eight shillings a week to buy it with, and the gentlemen present are too good arithmeticians to pretend that the sum comes out.
>
> Dr Gall has a paper. Leave wages where the farmers have put them, he says, and let the parish make every family up out of the rates, by a table: three shillings for the man, eighteenpence for his wife, eighteenpence for each child, the whole to rise and fall with the gallon loaf. “It is charity by the rule of three, gentlemen. It will cost the farmer nothing, and the labourer his impudence.” He looks down the cloth for a seconder, and his eye stops at you.

and, if you second him:

> The table is carried with two dissentients and ordered to be printed. Mr Hartopp has it by heart before the cloth is drawn, and has found in it what the Doctor did not put there: that if the parish will make a man up to nine shillings, the farmer is a fool who pays him above six. He says so to his neighbour, and does not lower his voice to say it. The paper goes to the Reading Mercury on Monday with your name second upon it.

What changed: the forty years are gone, because nobody at that table knew them. Hartopp working out the dodge before the cloth is drawn says the same thing from inside the room. The figures are the real ones of May 1795.

## A Hare in Evidence (speech by rank; the sum as the joke)

**Before**

> ...The law says that a man without land worth a hundred a year may not kill a hare, even one that walks in at his door.

and, if you let him off:

> You find the evidence insufficient, a look not being an offence known to English law. Mudge goes purple. Jem picks the hare up by the ears, considers it, and says, “I’d hang her four days, sir, no more, this weather.” It is the only thanks you get, and it is excellent advice.

**After**

> ...your uncle, who committed him twice, used to say there was not such another hand with a wire in Berkshire. The statute is five pounds, or three months. The hare might fetch three shillings.

> You find that a look is not evidence, and that a hare discovered in a man’s frock may, for anything the bench can prove, have got in of herself. Mudge turns the colour of a mulberry. Pryor takes the hare up by the ears and feels along her back. “Her wants four days’ hanging, sir, this weather, and not an hour over.” Then, nothing more being said, he puts her inside his frock again and makes his bow at the door, which Mudge does not hold for him.

## Fire in the Stackyard (the wall speaks)

**Before**

> The bill is posted on the church door and at the Bell. Nobody claims the money. Fifty guineas is more than a Hatherley man will see in three years, and still nobody claims it, and you find that this tells you more about your standing in the parish than the fire did.

**After**

> The bill is printed at Wyvern by noon, and pasted on the church door, at the Bell and at the turnpike gate. “FIFTY GUINEAS REWARD. Whereas some evil-disposed Person or Persons did, on the Night of Tuesday last, wilfully and maliciously set Fire to two Ricks of Wheat in the Stackyard of Thomas Ashcombe, Esquire; whoever will discover the Offender or Offenders, so that he or they be brought to Justice, shall on Conviction receive the above Reward. An Accomplice impeaching shall have the same, and Interest made for his Pardon.”
>
> Fifty guineas is three years’ wages to a Hatherley man. The bill hangs till the rain takes it down, and nobody comes. The wheat was worth three hundred pounds.

(The squire's name is the player's own; Ashcombe stands in here.)

## A quiet spring (the pocket-book)

**Before**

> Primroses in Cuckoo Copse, and a cuckoo, which arrives on the fourteenth of April as punctually as a bailiff.

**After**

> In your pocket-book, against the 14th of April: “Heard the cuckoo in the Copse, the same day as last year. Mudge had a shilling of me upon it. Paid the mole-catcher his quarter, 0. 10. 6. Dined on a couple of rabbits smothered with onions, and a gooseberry tart.”
