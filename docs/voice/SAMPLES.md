# Old beside new

Six events and eight quiet-season entries are in the Hatherley hand: `speen`, `flour`, `nore`, `thorneLease`, `poacher`, `rickFire`, and two vignettes a season. Each was cut down to its beats and written again from nothing. No flag, sum or id moved.

## The Pelican at Speen

**Old**

> The Berkshire bench has met at the Pelican Inn at Speen, over a turbot that cost more than a ploughman earns in a month, to decide what a ploughman ought to earn in a month. Nobody sees the joke.
>
> Dr Gall proposes that wages be left where they are and the parish make up the difference out of the rates, by a scale tied to the price of the gallon loaf: so much for a man, so much for his wife, so much for every child. “It costs us nothing,” he says, which is untrue, “and it keeps them quiet,” which may not be.

**New**

> The Pelican at Speen, the sixth of May. The Berkshire bench sits down to a turbot.
>
> The business is on a half-sheet beside each plate. Wheat is at seventy-five shillings. The quartern loaf is elevenpence in Newbury market. A labourer in this county has eight shillings a week. Dr Gall waits until the fish has been carried out before he stands.
>
> “Gentlemen, I shall not ask the farmer for a penny. Let wages lie. Let the parish make each family up from the rates, by a table. When the gallon loaf is a shilling, three shillings to the man and eighteenpence to every mouth he feeds. When the loaf rises, the table rises.” He holds his paper out over the cloth. “Who is with me?”
>
> Nobody moves. The Doctor turns his whole body in his chair until it faces you.
>
> “Mr Ashcombe?”

If you second him, **old**:

> ...You have helped build, over one dinner, a machine for turning labourers into paupers. It will run for forty years, and the turbot was overcooked.

**New**:

> “Seconded.” The show of hands takes less time than the carving did. A clerk is called in to copy the table fair for the Reading Mercury.
>
> Hartopp has a pencil out before the cheese comes. He writes on the back of his half-sheet, turns it round and pushes it across to you. “Parish makes a man up to 9s. I pay him 6s. Parish finds 3s.” Under that he has drawn a line. Under the line: “Why pay 6?”
>
> Your name goes to Reading second on the list.

## A Hare in Evidence

**Old**, if you let him off:

> You find the evidence insufficient, a look not being an offence known to English law. Mudge goes purple. Jem picks the hare up by the ears, considers it, and says, “I’d hang her four days, sir, no more, this weather.” It is the only thanks you get, and it is excellent advice.

**New**:

> “A look is not evidence, Mudge. Nobody saw him set the wire. Charge dismissed.” Mudge goes dark red to the ears. “Give him the hare.”
>
> Pryor picks her up and runs his thumb down her spine.
>
> “Four days I’d hang her, sir. This weather. No more.”
>
> He puts her back inside his frock and nods to you. Mudge will not open the door for him. Pryor opens it himself.

## The Widow of Long Barrow

**Old**

> Samuel Thorne has died of a chill, caught standing in his own water meadow arguing with a heron. His widow Abigail wishes to keep on the lease of Long Barrow: two hundred and forty acres, the best farm on the estate.
>
> Crabbe advises against. “A woman cannot farm, sir,” he says; and then, recollecting who has farmed Long Barrow these ten years while Samuel argued with herons, “not in the eyes of the county.”

**New**

> They buried Samuel Thorne on Tuesday. On Wednesday his widow is in your estate office at nine o’clock. She has brought the farm book.
>
> Long Barrow. Two hundred and forty acres. She opens the book on your desk and turns it to face you. Ten years of yields, all in one hand. It is not Samuel’s hand.
>
> “I want the lease in my name, sir. Same rent. Same terms.”
>
> Crabbe is standing behind your chair. He bends to your ear. “A woman tenant, sir. The county will not like it. And there is my nephew. A hundred pounds in hand, this morning, for the preference.”
>
> Mrs Thorne has good ears. She shuts the book and waits.

## A quiet summer, from the pocket-book

**Old**

> A cricket match on the common: Hatherley against Kintbury, for eleven pairs of gloves. The blacksmith bowls as if he held a grudge against the wicket personally.

**New**

> In your pocket-book: “Hatherley against Kintbury on the common, for eleven pair of gloves. Kintbury won by 14 notches. The smith bowled. He hit the wicket once and the Kintbury long-stop three times. Lost 0. 10. 6 to Mr Hartopp.”
